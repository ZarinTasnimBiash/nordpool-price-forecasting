Good morning. Fifteen minutes on a forecasting system for Swedish electricity prices — and the interesting parts are not these numbers. They are the four or five places where I measured something, got an answer I did not expect, and had to change a conclusion I had already written down.

The task: every day at eleven, before the Nord Pool auction closes at noon, predict tomorrow's twenty-four hourly prices for the Stockholm zone.

Result: MAE of nineteen and a half euros against twenty-six for the best simple rule. Twenty-five percent better, holding in all ten test months. I will spend much of this talk arguing that this number means less than it looks like it does.

Slide 02the task1:15

The whole problem is one deadline

Every feature must be something that genuinely existed at 11:00. Anything later is leakage — the model scores brilliantly in testing and fails in production.

The cutoff, and what exists on either side of it.
Say

This diagram is the whole problem. Everything else follows from it.

The orange line is eleven o'clock. Everything left of it exists; everything right of it does not.

The three rows behave differently, and that difference drove the design. Prices are easy — all of today's prices were published at one o'clock yesterday, so a twenty-four hour lag is safe. Weather observations stop dead at eleven. A twenty-four hour lag on an observation would, for tomorrow evening at six, reach a reading taken seven hours after my deadline. That is leakage.

The third row took two attempts to get right, and I will come back to it.

Slide 04the idea4:00

The distinction that was worth 3.9 EUR/MWh

Three things get lumped together as "weather". They are not the same, and my first design got this wrong.

StatusWhy
Past observed weather, up to 11:00VALIDgenuinely available
Tomorrow's actual observationsLEAKAGEonly knowable after tomorrow happens
A forecast for tomorrow, published before 11:00VALIDthe forecast existed at the cutoff
observed weather +0.07    forecast weather +3.89
Train on what you will deploy on. A model trained on perfect weather never learns to distrust that field, because in training it was never wrong. Deploy it against a forecast and it over-trusts a number that now carries error. So the training data must be forecasts including their errors — which is why an archive of forecasts as they were issued is the right data, not a re-forecast made today.
Say

The most important idea in the project, and I got it wrong first.

My original rule: I cannot know tomorrow's weather, therefore it is leakage, therefore exclude it. That sounds right. It is too broad, and it threw away the largest signal available.

Only tomorrow's observations are unavailable. A forecast for tomorrow published this morning, before my deadline, is a fact about today. It sat on a server. Using it is not leakage.

The measurement: observed weather worth nothing. Forecast weather worth nearly four euros — larger than the model's entire advantage over the baseline at that point.

The principle to remember: train on what you will deploy on. Train on perfect weather and the model never learns the field can be wrong. So I needed forecasts as they were issued historically, errors intact.

An automated leakage audit runs on every build

It refuses to write the table if any feature reaches past the cutoff. It caught three real bugs, none of which crashed anything:

  • A fixed 24-hour lag cannot escape a 25-hour daylight-saving day — it lands on the first hour of the same delivery day, a price from the same unpublished auction.
  • "One day earlier" computed as 24 elapsed hours drifted the cutoff to 12:00 on those same days, handing the model an extra hour.
  • A dropped timezone made a lookup match 0 of 1,083 rows, filling every weather column with nulls. Silently.
Say

Seventy features — price history, calendar, and the two kinds of weather.

What I want to talk about is the audit, because a leakage bug does not announce itself. It makes your model look better, quietly.

Three that happened. Sweden has a twenty-five hour day when the clocks go back; a fixed twenty-four hour lag cannot escape it, landing on the first hour of the same unpublished auction. Three hours in three years — I drop them.

Second: I computed "one day earlier" as twenty-four elapsed hours. On a twenty-five hour day that lands at one in the morning, so adding eleven gives noon. The model got an extra hour it would never have had.

Third, my favourite: a dropped timezone matched zero of one thousand and eighty-three rows. Every weather column came out empty. No error. Build reported success. That is why the builder now asserts no feature block is entirely null.

Slide 06the model7:15

How the model decides one real hour

Thursday 15 January 2026, 18:00 — evening peak, midwinter. The model had never seen this hour. Actual price: 104.42.

Each step is one tree adding a small correction. Read out of the real fitted model.
Say

Rather than describe gradient boosting abstractly — here is what it did on one real hour. Every number read out of the trained model.

It starts predicting the same thing for everything: the training average, thirty-nine euros. For this hour, wrong by sixty-six.

Then it adds trees. A tree is a chain of yes/no questions. The first asked three: was yesterday's price here below fifty-eight? No — a hundred and eight. Was the southern zone below a hundred and forty? No. Will it be windy in the south tomorrow? Yes. And it says: add two euros.

Two, not forty — every tree's answer is multiplied by the learning rate first. That timidity is the trick: one tree jumping the whole distance carries all its own mistakes; two hundred small steps let later trees correct earlier ones.

Note the third question is about tomorrow's forecast wind — slide four showing up inside the model.

Honest note: it finished at a hundred and fourteen, truth was a hundred and four, and the naive rule said a hundred and eight. On this hour the dumb rule won. That is why I picked a real example.

Slide 08the rule10:30

Is a difference real, or is it luck?

No fixed success threshold — any number I picked would have been set just under what I had already achieved. Instead, a rule I could not argue with afterwards.

keep a change only if it survives a
block bootstrap over whole days

Whole days, never individual hours. Neighbouring hours correlate about 0.95. Resampling hours independently would pretend there are 7,000 independent pieces of evidence when there are closer to 292 — and would wave through every change I ever made.

ComparisonΔ MAE95% CIVerdict
model vs best naive+6.72[+5.13, +8.42]real
hour-grouped vs single model−0.28[−0.79, +0.25]not distinguishable
Say

Before running anything I needed a rule for what counts as an improvement — otherwise you try twenty things, keep the best, and report it as truth.

I considered a target — "beat twenty euros" — and rejected it as circular. Any threshold would have been set just under what I already had.

Instead: resample the test days with replacement, two thousand times, and see how much the difference wobbles depending on which days you draw. Keep the change only if the interval excludes zero.

The critical detail is whole days. Resampling hours would treat seven thousand hours as seven thousand independent observations. They are not — neighbours are ninety-five percent alike. It is closer to three hundred. Hourly resampling would make everything look significant.

The control that reframed the whole project

"Gradient boosting beats the baseline by 25.6%" is ambiguous. Is that the algorithm — or the feature engineering, which any model would have benefited from?

difference +0.11, 95% CI [−0.72, +0.93] — not distinguishable from noise
Of the 6.72 the model gains over the naive baseline, 6.61 is available to a linear model on the same features. Almost all of the value came from the feature engineering and the leakage discipline — and almost none from the learning algorithm.

Boosting still ships, on a different argument. Not accuracy — stability. Ridge swings 14.4 to 24.2 across folds where boosting holds 16.5 to 22.2, and ridge's bad days are worse (p90 day 34.4 against 31.6).

Say

The slide I would most want you to push back on.

I ran the whole modelling stage, got twenty-five percent, wrote it up. Then I realised the claim was ambiguous. Is that the algorithm, or the feature engineering that any model would have benefited from? Nothing I had done separated the two.

So I ran a ridge regression. Same features, folds, discipline. Nineteen point six seven against nineteen point five six — a tenth of a euro, interval spanning zero.

Of the six point seven the model gains, six point six is available to a linear model. The value is in the feature engineering, not the algorithm. That is the opposite of where effort usually goes, which is why it was worth measuring rather than assuming.

I still ship boosting, but I had to change the argument — not more accurate, more consistent. "It's more accurate" would not have been defensible.

Failure 1 — a bug, and an invisible one

Early stopping graded every model with MAE. But MAE is minimised by the median, so judging a 90th-percentile model by MAE stops it exactly where it is least like a 90th percentile. Nothing crashed; the point forecast was untouched; the intervals looked like intervals. Only a coverage target fixed in advance caught it.

Failure 2 — not a bug at all

Quantile regression is overconfident by construction: its quantiles are fitted on rows the model has already seen, so they describe how wrong it was, not how wrong it will be. The fix measures rather than trusts — hold back recent data, see how far outside the interval reality actually fell, pad by exactly that much.

Say

A point forecast whose typical miss is a third of the price invites false confidence. So I wanted a range — and I set the standard before running anything: an interval claiming eighty percent and delivering fifty-five is worse than none.

First attempt: fifty-eight percent. Under my own bar.

The cause was my own bug, and how it hid matters. My early stopping asks whether validation error has stopped improving, measured with mean absolute error — for every model. But MAE is minimised by the median. So I was stopping the ninetieth-percentile model exactly where it was least like a ninetieth percentile. Six lines to fix. Coverage went to seventy.

The second problem is not a bug. Quantile regression is overconfident by construction — its quantiles are fitted on data it has already seen, so they describe how wrong it was. Every model is an optimist about itself.

The fix is blunt: stop trusting the interval, measure what it was missing on unseen data, add that back. Eighty point four against a target of eighty.

The honest cost is the width — sixty-nine euros against an average price near fifty-nine. But the narrower intervals were narrow by being wrong.

The most useful thing I found: the model shrinks

It over-predicts cheap hours and under-predicts expensive ones — regression to the mean. One mechanism explaining three symptoms I had been treating separately:

  • Why it adds nothing on spikes. Above €200 it under-predicts by 98 — saying ~154 when the truth is ~252. It is not failing to model spikes, it is shrinking them.
  • Why it adds nothing on negative hours. Same effect, other end.
  • Why the interval escapes through the ceiling more than the floor.
Say

The slide I would keep if I could keep only one from validation.

I had three separate problems written down. No value on spikes. No value on negative hours. And the interval missing high more than low. Three bullets, three investigations.

Then I plotted the signed error — direction, not size — against true price. They are all the same thing.

The model over-predicts cheap hours and under-predicts expensive ones. It pulls everything toward the middle. On the fifty-eight hours above two hundred it is under by ninety-eight — not failing to model the spike, actively shrinking it.

Two things against myself. I had written that the bias came from trees being unable to predict above their training maximum. I measured it: zero of seven thousand hours ever needed that. My explanation was wrong and I removed it. And I had quoted an aggregate bias of minus four and a half, which is misleading — it averages two opposite biases.

One more, about discipline rather than results.

Ten of my seventy features look useless — negative importance. So I ablated them, and dropping them improved my test folds by four tenths of a euro, interval clear of zero. A free improvement to the number I had just presented.

I did not take it.

First, procedural: I measured that on the reporting folds, which exist so no decision is made using them. "Keep things as they are" costs nothing. "Change something" means the holdout has now influenced the model, and it is no longer a holdout.

Second, decisive: the tuning folds — where I am allowed to decide — disagree in sign. Dropping them there is slightly harmful and not distinguishable from noise.

A change that helps in one period and hurts in another is unstable, not an improvement. I would rather report nineteen five six honestly than nineteen fifteen on something that will not replicate.

Slide 18Q&A prepbackup

Questions I expect — and the answers

Why not an LSTM, a transformer, or a proper time-series model?

Twenty thousand rows and seventy columns is squarely the regime where gradient boosting wins on tabular data; deep sequence models need far more data before they pull ahead. And the ridge control is the stronger answer — if a linear model ties boosting on these features, the marginal return from a more expressive model class is likely small. The binding constraint is information, not capacity. ARIMA-family models would also struggle with the 24-hours-ahead-in-one-shot structure and the exogenous weather.

Why MAE rather than RMSE?

Both are reported everywhere. MAE leads because the target's right tail reaches €707, so RMSE is dominated by a handful of spike hours and moves for reasons that have little to do with typical performance. Note this cuts against me: the shrinkage finding means RMSE is exactly where the model looks worst, and I show that table rather than hiding behind MAE.

How do you know there is no leakage left?

I do not know it — I have an automated audit that refuses to build the table if any feature's timestamp reaches past the cutoff, and it has caught three real bugs. That is evidence, not proof. The strongest indirect evidence is that the numbers are unglamorous: MAE is 33% of the price level. Leakage usually announces itself as a suspiciously good score.

You said boosting ties ridge — so why ship boosting?

Stability, explicitly not accuracy. Fold-to-fold spread 1.92 against 3.13, worst fold 22.2 against 24.2, p90 day 31.6 against 34.4. Ridge has both the best single fold and the worst. For a number someone bids against, consistency across regimes is the property worth having. If you disagree, ridge is a perfectly defensible choice and is in the repository.

Is a 69 EUR/MWh interval actually useful?

Honestly — marginal, and I say so in the write-up. It is calibrated at 80.4%, but it is about as wide as the price level. The width is the uncertainty, measured; narrowing it means a better model, not a different interval method. It is more useful as a statement about what is knowable 13–36 hours ahead than as a direct input to a bid.

The 62-day Nord Pool window — is that a production risk?

For training, it is why the second source exists. For production it is not a constraint: the daily job only ever fetches the newest day, which is always inside the window. The real production answer is the paid partner API, which is what the reference platform already uses.

How would this actually deploy?

The ingestion already runs on Dagster with daily partitions, retries and idempotent replace-per-partition writes — that half is production-shaped. What is missing is a scheduled inference asset at ~11:00, model versioning, and monitoring on realised error. Retraining monthly matches the fold design. The local DuckDB and parquet map directly onto BigQuery and cloud storage.

Did you check residual autocorrelation / is the bootstrap valid?

That is the reason the bootstrap resamples whole days rather than hours — neighbouring hours correlate ~0.95, so hourly resampling would overstate the evidence roughly 24-fold. Within-day residual correlation is absorbed by keeping the day intact as a block. Across days I treat the blocks as exchangeable, which the fold design supports but does not prove.

What is 6.7 EUR/MWh worth in money?

I deliberately have not converted it, because the answer depends on volume and on how the forecast enters the bidding decision — neither of which I have. What I can say is the shape: the gain is concentrated in ordinary hours and is zero at the extremes, so a naive "error × volume × hours" figure would overstate its value for spike-exposed positions.

Why forecast SE3 only?

Scope. All four zones are ingested and the other three are used as predictors — SE4 correlates 0.876 with SE3 and SE1 0.697. Extending the target to all four is mechanical, not conceptual, but it quadruples the modelling and reporting surface. It is on the open-questions list.

The market moved to 15-minute periods — why model hourly?

The warehouse stores the native resolution with a resolution_minutes column, so nothing is lost. The hourly grid averages quarter-hours into their hour. Three years of history is hourly, so modelling at 15 minutes would mean either discarding most of the history or fabricating sub-hourly detail for it. Worth revisiting once enough 15-minute history accumulates.
